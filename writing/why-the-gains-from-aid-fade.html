Why the Gains from Aid Fade

Liberia, Haiti, and Nigeria’s trade corridors show what happens when aid builds activity instead of capacity, and what lasting aid looks like.

By Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo, PhD · November 2026

When the United Nations mission left Liberia in 2018, it ended fifteen years of work that had helped hold a fragile peace. At its height, the mission brought nearly 17,000 foreign personnel into the country, and its spending filled hotels, offices, and markets in Monrovia. Within a year of its departure, the IMF’s growth forecast for Liberia fell from 4.7 percent to 0.4 percent, and most of the mission’s Liberian staff had not found new work. The peace held, yet much of the economy built around the mission went with it. Its spending had masked weak credit, poor infrastructure, and institutions that were never made strong enough to stand alone.

Liberia’s experience follows a familiar pattern. Aid often creates activity that looks like development, with new jobs, busy contracts, and rising trade figures, while the institutions, skills, and productive capacity that would keep that activity going remain thin. The gains are real while the money flows, and they fade when it stops. Three forces explain why this happens so often.

The first is that aid frequently serves the donor as much as the recipient. Tied aid requires recipients to buy equipment and services from the donor’s own firms, and large shares of many budgets go to consultants, reports, and travel that never reach the economy they are meant to help. After Haiti’s 2010 earthquake, a study by the Center for Economic and Policy Research found that only about one percent of traced American reconstruction funding went directly to Haitian companies, while a single Washington firm received seventeen percent. Years earlier, cheap imported American rice, helped by low tariffs and food aid, had undercut Haitian rice farmers, a policy former President Bill Clinton later called a mistake. Allocation is shaped by donor interests too. Aid tends to flow towards strategic allies and towards countries that trade with donors or send migrants to them, which leaves many poor countries with average prospects and little leverage at the back of the queue.

The second force is that aid often bypasses the institutions it should be building. When donors distrust a government, they route money through their own agencies and non-governmental organisations. That choice can protect funds in the short term, yet it leaves public institutions no stronger and sometimes weaker, as skilled staff move to better-paid project jobs. Haiti came to be called a republic of NGOs for this reason. The reverse failure matters as well. Aid has propped up governments that served donor interests while neglecting their own people, as it did for decades in Mobutu’s Zaire. In both cases, the institutions that ordinary people depend on were left out of the bargain.

The third force is the hardest to see. Even well-designed aid can have its gains absorbed by the system it enters. Research on aid for trade in Nigeria, following goods along the Lagos-Kano-Jibiya corridor, found a striking tension. Aid helped improve export performance, while the cost of trading kept rising, because checkpoints, informal payments, slow procedures, and weak governance captured much of the benefit. Aid lifted the headline numbers, and the structures that taxed every truck on the road stayed in place. When a programme ends in such conditions, the costs remain and the gains slip away.

These questions have become urgent. In 2025, the United States cancelled most of USAID’s programmes and closed the agency on 1 July, moving its remaining work into the State Department. Countries that had come to rely on that funding are now learning what Liberia learned in 2018. Dependence is itself a risk, and the cost of that risk falls hardest on the poorest households.

Aid that lasts looks different. It is untied, so that money builds local firms and local skills. It belongs to the recipient, with the recipient’s government or accountable local institutions in charge and credible monitoring and evaluation to keep everyone honest. It invests in infrastructure that connects producers to markets and in businesses that create jobs, so that countries trade more and rely on aid less. It strengthens the institutions it works through instead of building parallel ones. It plans its own exit from the first day. South Korea shows that this path is possible. Heavily dependent on aid in the 1950s and 1960s, it built its own industries, schools, and institutions, and in 2010 it joined the OECD’s Development Assistance Committee as a donor.

Aid works best as a bridge, a temporary support that helps a country build the capacity to carry itself. Too often it becomes a scaffold that holds up an economy from the outside and leaves nothing standing when it comes down. The true measure of any aid programme is what remains after the donors go home.
