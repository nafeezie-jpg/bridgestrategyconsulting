What Nigeria Can Learn from Asia’s Rise

China, India, and the East Asian economies took different roads out of poverty. What they shared matters more than what they copied.

By Dr. Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo · November 2026

In 1960, South Korea and Ghana had roughly the same income per person. Both were poor, both had been shaped by colonial rule, and neither looked like a future economic power. Within two generations, South Korea had become one of the world’s richest economies and a donor of aid. China’s story is larger still. According to the World Bank, the number of Chinese people living in extreme poverty fell by close to 800 million over forty years. These are the most dramatic escapes from poverty in modern history, and Nigeria has every reason to study them closely.

The first thing the record shows is that there was no single Asian model. India began with the Green Revolution of the 1960s, which ended its recurring famines, and later opened its economy in the reforms of 1991. China started in 1978 with rural reforms that let farm households sell surplus produce, then opened special economic zones on its coast. South Korea and Taiwan redistributed land, built industries behind protective walls, and then pushed those industries to compete in world markets. Their political systems ranged from India’s democracy to one-party rule in China. The roads differed, yet the same few lessons appear along each of them.

The first lesson is that the countryside came first. Land reform in South Korea and Taiwan gave millions of families a stake in their own farms. China’s rural reforms raised farm output and incomes before its factories took off, and India’s Green Revolution made the country able to feed itself. Higher rural incomes created demand for manufactured goods, freed labour for industry, and kept food cheap for growing cities. Growth that leaves the majority of people behind in poverty rarely lasts long enough to transform an economy.

The second lesson is that people were treated as the main investment. The East Asian economies put children in school and kept them healthy long before they became rich, so that when factories arrived, a literate and healthy workforce was ready for them. China did the same on an enormous scale, giving hundreds of millions of young people basic education and health care that later powered its industrial rise. Nigeria has one of the youngest populations in the world. Whether that youth becomes a dividend or a burden depends almost entirely on the schools, skills, and jobs available to it.

The third lesson concerns how industry was built. Nigeria tried import substitution in the 1970s and 1980s, protecting local industries from foreign competition, yet many protected firms grew comfortable and inefficient, and the policy is now widely seen as a failure. The East Asian economies protected their industries too. The difference was discipline. Governments in South Korea and Taiwan tied credit, tax breaks, and protection to export performance, so firms that could not compete abroad lost their support. World markets became the judge, and only the firms that improved survived. Protection worked when it came with conditions and an end date.

The fourth lesson is that stability and capable institutions held everything together. The Asian success stories kept inflation and exchange rates under control, managed the flow of capital in and out of their economies, and invested steadily in roads, ports, and power. They also built competent agencies to plan and coordinate development, staffed by officials who were judged on results. Nigeria’s repeated currency crises and unreliable power supply show how costly the absence of these foundations can be. Investors and entrepreneurs plan around stability, and they hesitate wherever it is missing.

The final lesson may be the most important. None of these countries copied another wholesale. Each borrowed ideas, tested them, and adapted them to its own history, institutions, and resources. China’s reformers spoke of crossing the river by feeling the stones, moving step by step and keeping what worked. Nigeria cannot simply transplant a Korean or Chinese model into a federation of more than two hundred million people, with an oil economy and deep regional differences. It can borrow the principles and build its own version of them.

Taken together, these lessons point to a clear agenda. Raise the productivity and incomes of rural households. Invest heavily in the education, health, and skills of young Nigerians. Support industries that can compete, and hold them to the standard of export markets, which the African Continental Free Trade Area now makes far larger. Keep the economy stable and invest in power and transport. Build public agencies that are capable, accountable, and judged by results. Asia’s rise shows that a country can escape mass poverty within a single lifetime. It also shows that doing so takes a generation of consistent choices, made by people who expect to be measured by what those choices achieve.

Sources: World Bank, Four Decades of Poverty Reduction in China (2022); historical income comparisons for Ghana and South Korea; Alice Amsden, Asia’s Next Giant (1989), and Joe Studwell, How Asia Works (2013), on export discipline in East Asian industrial policy.
