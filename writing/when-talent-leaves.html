When Talent Leaves: Migration as a Development Question

Nigerians abroad send home far more than the country receives in aid, while its hospitals lose their doctors and nurses. Policy has to manage both sides of the same movement.

By Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo, PhD · November 2026

Nigeria has a single word for leaving. Japa, borrowed from Yoruba slang for fleeing in a hurry, now describes the steady departure of young professionals for the United Kingdom, Canada, the United States, and beyond. The numbers behind the word are striking. The Nigerian Association of Resident Doctors reports that 3,974 doctors left the country in 2024 alone, from a workforce of about 55,000 licensed doctors. The national nurses’ association says around 16,000 nurses moved to the United Kingdom in 2025. In the same year, Nigerians abroad sent home about $21.8 billion, according to the Central Bank of Nigeria, far more than the country receives in official aid. Migration is one of the largest development forces acting on Nigeria, yet it is rarely treated as one.

Migration itself is old and ordinary. People have always moved in search of safety, work, and opportunity, and in doing so they have spread ideas, built trade, and driven innovation. Ian Goldin, Geoffrey Cameron, and Meera Balarajan make this case in Exceptional People, and Hein de Haas has shown that the share of the world’s population living outside its country of birth is roughly what it was a century ago. Decisions to migrate are rarely impulsive. Families weigh costs and risks, networks abroad open paths, and conditions in both countries push and pull. Policies that only try to close borders tend to push people towards smugglers and dangerous routes instead of keeping them home.

For the countries people leave, migration brings real gains. Remittances go straight to households, where they pay school fees, hospital bills, rent, and the start-up costs of small businesses. They are steadier than aid, they arrive without conditions, and they reach families that development programmes often miss. Migrants also bring back knowledge, investment, and connections. Economists call this brain circulation, and it has helped countries such as India and China build industries with the skills and networks of their diasporas.

The costs are just as real. When doctors, nurses, engineers, and teachers leave faster than they can be replaced, the country that trained them loses the people it most needs to grow. Nigeria pays to educate its health workers, and richer countries capture the return on that investment. The losses fall hardest where services were already thin. Lagos has more than 7,000 licensed doctors, while Taraba State has about 200. In 2023, the World Health Organization placed Nigeria among 55 countries with the most pressing shortages of health workers, and the United Kingdom added it to its list of countries where active recruitment is discouraged. The departures continued anyway, because the reasons for leaving remained.

Those reasons deserve attention. Most skilled Nigerians leave because of insecurity, low pay, poor working conditions, unreliable services, and limited prospects, and few return while those conditions persist. Poverty drives a different kind of migration. People without savings, education, or networks abroad have no safe options, so some turn to traffickers and perilous journeys across the desert and the Mediterranean. Climate change adds further pressure, as droughts and floods push rural families from land that can no longer support them.

Donor governments have their own stake in these flows. Research by Sarah Bermeo and David Leblang shows that aid increasingly goes to countries that send migrants to donors, as a way of managing migration at its source. The result can be uneven. Countries that send fewer migrants, or that trade less with donors, may be left with less support, even when their needs are just as great. At the same time, the receiving countries that fund development programmes often recruit the very health workers those programmes are trying to keep.

A serious migration policy for Nigeria would treat the movement of people as part of its development strategy. The first task is development at home, with the jobs, security, pay, and public services that make staying a real choice. Remittances can be put to work by cutting the cost of sending money, expanding diaspora bonds and investment schemes, and making it easier for Nigerians abroad to start businesses at home. Training partnerships could ask the countries that recruit Nigerian health workers to share the cost of training their replacements. Return and circulation can be encouraged through short-term placements, recognition of foreign experience, and roles that use returnees’ skills. Safe and legal routes abroad would reduce the trade in human beings.

Amartya Sen described development as the expansion of the real freedoms people enjoy. Those freedoms include the freedom to move and the freedom to stay. Nigeria’s goal should be a country its people can leave by choice and return to with opportunity, where talent flows both ways and each departure can become a link instead of a loss.
