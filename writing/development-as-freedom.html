Development as Freedom: What Nigeria’s Poverty Figures Miss

What Amartya Sen teaches about poverty, freedom, and the real measure of progress in Nigeria.

By Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo, PhD · November 2026

For decades, development was measured by a single number. A country was succeeding if its gross domestic product was rising, and the main question for policy was how to make it rise faster. Growth matters, and no poor country has escaped poverty without it. Yet a rising GDP can sit beside empty classrooms, clinics without staff, and families who cannot count on their next meal. Nigeria knows this well. In 2022, the National Bureau of Statistics found that 133 million Nigerians, 63 percent of the population, were multidimensionally poor, deprived in health, living standards, and access to food. Only 40 percent were poor by the national income measure. The gap between those two figures is the space where so much of Nigeria’s poverty hides.

Amartya Sen offered a better measure. In Development as Freedom, he describes development as the process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy. Freedom here is practical. It means being able to avoid starvation and preventable illness, to read and write, to take part in public life, and to make choices about one’s own future. Sen gives freedom two roles. It is the end that development aims at, and it is also the main means of getting there, because people who are healthy, educated, and free to act are the ones who build prosperous economies.

Sen names five kinds of freedom that work together, namely political freedoms, economic facilities, social opportunities, transparency guarantees, and protective security. Each supports the others. Markets give people economic room, yet a trader who cannot read a contract or a worker who cannot reach a clinic cannot use that room fully. Schools and health care build capability, yet they do little for a young graduate with no job to go to. Seen this way, poverty is a web of missing freedoms, and pulling on a single thread rarely frees anyone.

Deprivations also feed on one another. Hunger weakens health, illness keeps children out of school, and a family without savings is one emergency away from losing what little it has. A missing freedom in one area quietly closes doors in the others. The reverse is also true. A secure job, a reliable meal, a nearby clinic, and a good school reinforce one another, and together they give a household the footing to plan beyond the next crisis. Food, health, education, and work are all basic freedoms in this sense, and each is a means to the larger end of a life people have reason to value.

Sen’s lens also explains why freedom is shared so unevenly within a country. In many communities, women face limits on owning property, inheriting land, making household decisions, and finishing school. Restoring those freedoms is good for women, and it is one of the most direct routes to higher incomes and healthier children. The same holds for young people. UNICEF reports that only one in four Nigerian schoolchildren can read properly and do basic mathematics by the age of fourteen. A generation without those skills has fewer choices, and so does the economy it will inherit.

The countries that made the fastest progress in the last century understood this, even when they did not use Sen’s language. The East Asian economies invested heavily in schooling, health, and broad-based incomes early in their growth, so their people could seize the jobs that industry later created. China’s rise rested on similar foundations of basic education and health for hundreds of millions of people. Their paths differed in many ways, and none can be copied whole. What they shared was the decision to build human capability first and let growth draw on it.

Institutions decide whether any of this happens. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson have shown how inclusive economic and political institutions allow societies to grow and share the gains, while extractive ones concentrate both power and wealth. Freedom cannot expand where courts are weak, public money disappears, or citizens have no voice. Development assistance has often stumbled at exactly this point. Aid tied to donor interests, spent on consultants and reports, or delivered without regard for local institutions can leave a country no freer than before. Aid works best when it strengthens the institutions and capabilities that let a country stand on its own.

For Nigeria, the lesson is practical. Progress should be judged by whether more Nigerians can eat well, learn, stay healthy, earn a fair living, and shape the decisions that affect them. Jobs, education, health, food, and governance belong to one agenda, since a gain in one is quickly lost without the others. Each region will also need its own path. Multidimensional poverty touches 27 percent of people in Ondo and 91 percent in Sokoto, and no single programme can serve both equally well. Measured this way, development stops being an abstraction on a budget line. It becomes the steady widening of what ordinary people are free to do and to become.

This essay is developed from the author’s graduate writing on human development and development theory, 2020 to 2021.

Sources: Amartya Sen, Development as Freedom (1999); Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson, Why Nations Fail (2012); National Bureau of Statistics, Nigeria Multidimensional Poverty Index (2022), reported by Nairametrics; UNICEF remarks on foundational learning, reported by News Times (June 2026).
