Security Is Measured in Lives

Human security asks a simple question of governments and global institutions alike: are people safe from fear and from want? The record from Srebrenica to Nigeria shows how often the answer falls short.

By Dr. Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo · November 2026

In 1994, the United Nations Development Programme proposed a new way of thinking about security. For most of the twentieth century, security had meant the protection of states, their borders, and their governments. The 1994 Human Development Report argued that security should also be measured in the lives of ordinary people, in their freedom from fear and their freedom from want. It named seven dimensions of human security, covering economic, food, health, environmental, personal, community, and political security. A country can keep its borders intact while its people go hungry, fall sick without care, or live in fear of violence. By the human security standard, that country is far from secure.

The idea has aged well, because the threats that kill the most people rarely respect borders. Hunger, disease, displacement, and violence against civilians cause more suffering than wars between states. The COVID-19 pandemic made this plain. A virus that began in one city reached the whole world within months, and the global response exposed how fragile cooperation can be. Dozens of governments restricted exports of masks, gloves, and ventilators, and poorer countries waited at the back of the queue for vaccines. Senegal offered a hopeful contrast, drawing on its experience with Ebola to develop affordable testing for itself and its neighbours.

Global institutions were built for a world of states, and they often struggle to protect people. The UN Security Council can act only when its permanent members agree, and their interests frequently decide what happens. The cost of that limitation was clearest at Srebrenica in July 1995, when around 8,000 Bosnian Muslim men and boys were killed in an area the UN had declared safe, while lightly armed peacekeepers stood by. In 2007, the International Court of Justice found that Serbia had failed to prevent genocide. For the survivors, many of whom still wait for the remains of their relatives to be identified, that judgment came late and offered little.

The Responsibility to Protect was the world’s answer to such failures. Adopted at the 2005 World Summit, it holds that every state must protect its people from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity, that other states should help it do so, and that the international community may act collectively when a state manifestly fails. The principle has been tested hard. In 2017, more than 700,000 Rohingya Muslims fled Myanmar for Bangladesh after a military campaign of killings and burnings, following decades in which the Myanmar state had stripped them of citizenship and basic rights. The Security Council remained divided. It was The Gambia, a small West African state, that brought a genocide case against Myanmar to the International Court of Justice in 2019, showing that the protection of people can be championed from unexpected places.

Human security also exposes threats that conventional security misses entirely. Amartya Sen calculated in 1990 that more than 100 million women were missing from the world’s population, lost to sex-selective abortion, neglect of girls, and unequal access to food and care. Violence against women, in its many forms, remains one of the most widespread threats to human security anywhere. It rarely appears on a Security Council agenda, and it shapes the lives of hundreds of millions of people.

Nigeria shows how the dimensions of human security reinforce one another. Banditry, insurgency, and kidnapping have spread across large parts of the country. Save the Children reports that at least 1,683 students were kidnapped and 184 killed in attacks on schools between 2014 and 2022. Clashes between farmers and herders displace families and disrupt planting. Insecurity keeps farmers from their fields, which raises food prices and deepens hunger, and hunger and displacement in turn feed recruitment into armed groups. A response that treats each of these problems separately will struggle, because they share the same roots in poverty, exclusion, weak institutions, and a changing climate.

Liberia and Iraq offer a further lesson about how institutions protect people over time. In Liberia, a UN mission helped hold a fragile peace for fifteen years, yet the institutions that should have sustained it were left too weak, and the economy faltered when the mission left. In Iraq, the UN’s Oil-for-Food programme, meant to protect civilians under sanctions, was undermined by corruption and weak oversight. In both places, security without strong and accountable institutions proved hard to sustain.

Better protection starts with prevention. It means investing in the conditions that keep people safe, including jobs, schools, health care, food, and fair justice, long before crises erupt. It means strengthening regional bodies such as the African Union and ECOWAS, which are often closest to emerging crises, and funding the institutions of fragile states as well as the missions sent to stabilise them. It means counting women and girls, listening to affected communities, and treating hunger, disease, and displacement as security threats in their own right. Security, in the end, is measured in lives, in whether people can sleep without fear, eat without worry, and plan for a future they can reach.
