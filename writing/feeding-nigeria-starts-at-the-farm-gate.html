Feeding Nigeria Starts at the Farm Gate

Why Nigerian farmers stay poor while the country imports food, and what the value chain has to do with it.

By Nafeesat Rabiu-Adebayo, PhD · November 2026

A field of maize in Nigeria can look healthy from the road and still tell a story of loss. The farmer standing in it may have planted late because the fertiliser came late. He may have used half the recommended dose because the price climbed on its way to him, and he will likely sell his harvest at the farm gate within weeks because he has nowhere to store it. Each of those gaps has a cause far from his field. Together, they keep him poor.

Nigeria still tends to treat food security as a question of production, of how many more tonnes the country can grow. That framing misses where so much of the value is lost. Crops and livestock make up about a fifth of the economy, and millions of households live from them, yet the Cadre Harmonisé projected that 34.7 million Nigerians would face acute food insecurity between June and August 2026. A country can grow more and still go hungry when the chain between the input supplier and the dinner table breaks at almost every link.

The chain breaks first at inputs. Nigerian farmers apply about 15 kilogrammes of fertiliser per hectare, according to the head of the country’s largest fertiliser producer, far below the 50 kilogramme target African governments committed to years ago. Price explains part of the gap, and timing explains the rest. Under national fertiliser programmes, a two-week delay on the road becomes a lost season on the farm, since a crop that misses its planting window cannot wait for the paperwork. Fertiliser, seed, and crop protection also work together, yet programmes tend to subsidise one and forget the others. A subsidised bag of fertiliser spread on poor seed is money scattered on the soil.

The better answer is to deliver inputs as a package, with advice attached. OCP Africa’s Agribooster programme offers a useful model. It bundled seed, fertiliser, and insecticide into one subsidised package and sold it through one-stop shops staffed by extension agents across northern states. The lesson is simple. Inputs work when a farmer receives them together, on time, and with someone nearby who knows how to use them. Nigeria’s extension service carries that knowledge to the village, and it has been stretched thin for years. No input programme can succeed until it is rebuilt.

The chain breaks again after harvest. Most smallholders sell at the farm gate because they lack storage, processing, and reliable transport, so they accept whatever the off-taker offers before the crop spoils. A Nigerian saying captures it well, that the monkey works and the baboon eats. The farmer carries all the risk of the season, while the margin goes to whoever controls the next step. Until farmers can store, process, or sell together, higher yields will mostly enrich the middle of the chain.

Further along, the chain runs into the road itself. Research on agricultural trade along the Lagos-Kano-Jibiya corridor points to an uncomfortable pattern. Aid helped exports grow while the cost of trading rose, because checkpoints, informal payments, and slow procedures absorbed much of the gain before it reached the people who grew the goods. Inputs meet the same walls across West Africa. Each country runs its own testing and registration for seed and fertiliser, so a blend approved in one market can wait months to enter the next. Farmers respond by trading informally, which keeps them outside the protection and finance of the formal market.

Every link in this chain has a person standing at it, and too often those people are left out of the decisions. Women lead much of the processing and trading in Nigerian communities and know which crops will sell, yet they are rarely in the room when programmes are designed. Young people adopt new practices faster than their parents and could carry an innovation across a whole village, yet extension services seldom recruit them. Bringing farmers, women, and youth into planning from the start costs little, and it is one of the surest improvements any ministry can make.

The case for reform fits in a single paragraph. Deliver inputs as a bundle, on a calendar set by the planting season. Rebuild extension so that advice travels with every bag. Put storage and small-scale processing close to farms, so farmers can wait for a fair price. Harmonise input standards across ECOWAS so that good seed and fertiliser move freely, and cut the informal costs that tax every truck on the corridors. Then judge success by what reaches the farmer, in income and in food on the table, rather than by what leaves the warehouse.

All of this matters because food security is a means to something larger. Amartya Sen described development as the expansion of the real freedoms people enjoy, and a household that can eat well, keep its children in school, and plan beyond the next harvest has more of those freedoms than one that cannot. The countries that escaped mass poverty, from India after its Green Revolution to China and the East Asian economies, began by making their farmers more productive and better connected. Nigeria can walk that path too. It starts at the farm gate, with a chain built to carry the gains all the way to the farmer.
