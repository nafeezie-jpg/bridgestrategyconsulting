The Problem I Work On

The Last Mile

Too many farmer programs are designed far from the field, and they discover too late that the plan never fit real life. I am the voice that belongs in the room while the program is still being shaped, the one with the ground experience to know what will work, the rigor to prove whether it did, and the clarity to make the whole thing land.

The world already grows enough food, and it already knows how to farm. Hunger and rural poverty survive somewhere quieter, in the last mile between what institutions produce and what actually reaches the farmer. That distance is where food security is decided, and few people have worked it from every side. I have trained smallholder farmers as an extension officer, moved fertilizer at national scale, built technology meant to cross the gap, and evaluated the aid designed to close it.

My doctoral research named one version of the problem. Aid can lift a country's exports while quietly raising the cost of trading, so the gains register at the institution and are absorbed before they reach the people who grew the goods. Who captures that value, and why, is a question of politics and institutions rather than agronomy. The pattern repeats wherever food moves, and the work, every time, is to close the distance.

Research

The Aid-for-Trade Paradox

My doctoral research named and evidenced a paradox at the heart of aid policy, building an original dataset from scratch and combining a Theory of Change evaluation with trade-cost and corridor analysis. The finding was that aid can improve a country's export performance while raising its trade costs, as governance, informal costs, and institutional behaviour absorb the gains along the way. It carries direct implications for how development finance is targeted and measured.
University of East Anglia · School of Global Development · 2025
Point of View

What I argue.

The positions my work is built on, applied to the questions food systems are facing right now.

01
Too much of what farmers grow is lost before it reaches the market.
For perishable crops, inadequate storage, unreliable transport, and gaps in cold-chain infrastructure can cause substantial losses between harvest and sale. Improving agricultural production matters, but so does protecting what farmers have already grown.
02
Aid can improve export performance while leaving the costs of trading stubbornly high.
My doctoral research examined this tension in Nigeria's agricultural trade system, showing how institutional arrangements, governance, and trade costs complicate the relationship between development assistance and its intended economic outcomes.
03
Agricultural innovation means little when farmers cannot access what already works.
