The problem I work on

The last mile

Too many farmer programs are designed far from the field, and they discover too late that the plan never fit real life.

The world already grows enough food, and it already knows how to farm. Hunger and rural poverty survive in the last mile between what institutions produce and what actually reaches the farmer. That distance is where food security is decided. I have worked it from every side: training smallholder farmers as an extension officer, moving fertilizer at national scale, building technology meant to cross the gap, and evaluating the aid designed to close it.

My doctoral research named one version of the problem. Aid can lift a country's exports while quietly raising the cost of trading, so the gains register at the institution and are absorbed before they reach the people who grew the goods.

Four places a program breaks on the way down

Timing

Does the input arrive before the planting window closes? A missed season is a lost year.

Reach

Who carries it the final stretch, over which road, in whose vehicle? This is where delivery plans quietly stop being true.

Cost

Who captures the margin between the farmgate and the market, and which costs were made by policy rather than geography?

Capability

Is anyone there to advise the farmer when it lands? An input without advice is a cost the household carries alone.

Evaluating a $16.6M USAID trade project

I evaluated USAID's NEXTT trade project along the Lagos, Kano, and Jibiya corridor, building the framework, methodology, and dataset from scratch because none existed.

The process evaluation, grounded in a theory of change, was paired with trade-cost and export analysis and interviews across government, logistics, and trade. It surfaced the Aid-for-Trade Paradox: aid improved export performance while trade costs rose, as governance and informal costs absorbed the gains.

Point of view

What I argue

The positions my work is built on, applied to the questions food systems face right now.

Too much of what farmers grow is lost before it reaches the market.

For perishable crops, weak storage, unreliable transport, and gaps in cold chains cause heavy losses between harvest and sale. Protecting what farmers already grow matters as much as growing more.

Aid can improve export performance while trade costs stay stubbornly high.

My doctoral research examined this tension in Nigeria's agricultural trade system, showing how institutions, governance, and trade costs complicate the link between aid and its intended outcomes.

Agricultural innovation means little when farmers cannot reach what already works.

Improved seed, the right fertilizer, and reliable advice raise productivity only when farmers can get them affordably, on time, and with support to use them well.

Food security is shaped in the last mile, where institutions meet households.

Research, finance, and policy set the conditions for progress. Implementation decides how much of it reaches the people it was meant for.
